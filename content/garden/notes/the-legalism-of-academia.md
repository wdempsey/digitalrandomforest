Academia has a legalism problem.

This is related to Goodhart's law, but it is not exactly the same thing. Goodhart's law says: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The problem there is that a measurable proxy gets promoted into the goal itself.

Academic legalism is a neighboring failure mode. Here the issue is not only that measurable things become bad goals. It is that soft norms, contextual judgments, and discretionary practices get hardened into rigid rules because institutions want consistency, fairness, auditability, and protection against error.

Those goals are understandable. Soft rules can be abused. Informal judgment can hide favoritism. Discretion can produce unequal treatment. So the institution responds by converting judgment into procedure.

But something is lost in that conversion.

The original norm may have been trying to preserve an unmeasured value: intellectual seriousness, mentorship, intellectual generosity, careful review, community trust, humane flexibility, or professional judgment. Once the norm becomes a rule, people begin optimizing for compliance with the rule rather than preservation of the value. The rule becomes easier to administer, but the value becomes harder to see.

This is the non-measurable cousin of Goodhart's law. Goodhart warns that what can be measured can become a bad goal. Academic legalism warns that what cannot be measured can be flattened into rules that slowly stop serving the original purpose.

## Working Distinction

Goodhart's law:

- A measure becomes a target.
- The target stops measuring what mattered.
- The system optimizes the proxy.

Academic legalism:

- A soft norm becomes a rule.
- The rule stops preserving the value that motivated it.
- The system optimizes procedural compliance.

The first failure is about bad proxies. The second is about brittle governance.

## Examples to Develop

- Recommendation letters become formulaic evidence packets rather than acts of judgment.
- Mentoring expectations become checklists that can be completed without real mentorship.
- Research integrity processes become compliance rituals rather than cultures of honest inquiry.
- Hiring rubrics reduce arbitrary treatment but can also suppress intellectual risk-taking.
- DEI, ethics, and reproducibility norms become paperwork rather than serious commitments.
- Authorship and contribution rules create clarity but can miss the real texture of intellectual labor.

## Why This Hurts

Legalism makes institutions legible to themselves, but it can make them less intelligent.

A good academic institution needs judgment at multiple levels: faculty judgment, student judgment, editorial judgment, peer-review judgment, administrative judgment, and field-level judgment about what kinds of work matter. When every soft norm becomes a rule, the institution reduces the need to think. It gets consistency by narrowing the space where judgment can operate.

That tradeoff may be necessary in some places. But if it becomes the default posture, academia becomes a compliance machine that is bad at preserving the very values it claims to protect.

## Connection to AI

AI will intensify this problem.

When academic work becomes easier to generate, institutions will be tempted to respond with more formal rules, more checklists, more verification systems, more declarations, and more procedural safeguards. Some of that will be necessary. But if the response to AI is only legalistic, academia will protect itself by making itself less thoughtful.

The better response is to design institutions that preserve judgment, not merely enforce compliance.

## Open Questions

- Which academic rules genuinely protect values, and which mainly protect institutions from ambiguity?
- Where does consistency improve fairness, and where does it erase the judgment needed for fairness?
- What kinds of academic discretion are worth defending?
- How do we design AI-era academic norms that are inspectable without becoming purely legalistic?
- What would a non-legalistic accountability system look like?
