Academics often think they are already developing in public.

They post preprints, give talks, release code, write occasional blog posts, and argue on social media. That is public in one sense. But in the age of AI, it is not transparent enough.

AI changes the relevant unit of research communication. The artifact is no longer only the paper, the code repository, or the polished blog post. The artifact increasingly includes the environment that produced the work: prompts, evaluation harnesses, agent workflows, failed attempts, model comparisons, data checks, review loops, and the scaffolding that lets another person understand how judgment was applied.

That points toward a stronger norm: radical transparency.

Radical transparency does not mean broadcasting every private thought or confusing rough work with finished claims. It means making the research process legible enough that others can see how an idea was formed, tested, revised, and sometimes abandoned. It means being willing to be wrong in public, not as performance, but because visible correction is part of how fields learn.

For AI-native research, this should include more than blogs. It should include shared harnesses, reproducible workflows, prompt-and-evaluation traces, structured notes, small demos, and explicit accounts of what did not work. A serious AI-era research culture needs artifacts that expose the *loop*, not only the final result.

Kyla Scanlon makes a related point in [[https://kyla.substack.com/p/is-ai-going-to-destroy-our-lives|Is AI Going to Destroy Our Lives or Not?]], where her practical advice for jobs in the age of AI includes building something in public because networks increasingly come from a visible portfolio of work. That framing matters for academics too. Engineers, designers, marketers, and creators have long understood portfolios as proof of taste, judgment, and execution. Academic work still leans too heavily on credentials, papers, and institutional affiliation. But if AI weakens old credentialing signals and makes generic output cheaper, then public artifacts become more important: notes, demos, replications, workflows, talks, harnesses, and visible intellectual taste.

This also changes how statistics should relate to neighboring fields.

I have a strong reaction against the old "play in other people's backyards" mentality. Collaboration across economics, computer science, social science, public health, and engineering is essential. But statistics cannot only show up as a method supplier after someone else has defined the problem, built the system, and framed the stakes. That posture makes the field reactive. It trains statisticians to contribute tools without shaping the intellectual environment in which those tools matter.

Radical transparency is one way out. If statisticians build in public, share AI-native environments, explain their reasoning loops, and engage seriously with adjacent fields while problems are still being formed, then the field is not merely visiting someone else's backyard. It is helping design the shared terrain.

## Working Claim

In the AI era, "developing in public" is too weak a norm for academic work. The stronger norm is radical transparency: sharing enough of the research environment, reasoning loop, failures, and communication process that others can learn from how the work was made, not only from what survived into the final paper.

## What Radical Transparency Might Include

- Blogs that explain live questions before they become papers.
- AI-native research harnesses that let others inspect workflows, evaluations, and model interactions.
- Living portfolios of notes, demos, talks, replications, and prototypes that show taste and judgment before formal publication.
- Public mistakes and reversals that show how judgment changes.
- Communication with economics, computer science, social science, and domain fields while the question is still unstable.
- Notes that expose uncertainty rather than hiding it behind polished conclusions.
- Reusable environments that make research practice easier to inherit, not only research findings easier to cite.

- What norms would make being wrong in public intellectually productive rather than reputationally punitive?
